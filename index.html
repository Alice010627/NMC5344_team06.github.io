Usage intensity stands out

The exploratory analysis found a clear negative association between daily social media usage and mental health scores. Differences by purpose of use and platform were less pronounced overall.

Platform comparisons can be affected by small subgroup sizes; the report focuses visual comparisons on platforms with at least 100 observations.

Sleep and study are positively associated

Exploratory plots showed positive trends for study hours, sleep and physical activity. After adjusting for other variables, sleep and study remained significant in the main-effects regression, while physical activity did not.

Subgroup differences are nuanced

Gender differences were generally small in several comparisons. Academic level showed more noticeable differences in selected relationships, while age did not show a consistent moderating pattern in exploratory analysis.

A closely related well-being indicator

Stress level was strongly negatively correlated with mental health score (Spearman’s ρ = −0.792). The study treated stress as a separate indicator rather than an independent predictor in its main regression models.

04 / Interpretation

Important caveats.

The dataset was sourced from Kaggle, but the report notes that sampling, measurement and collection-period details were not sufficiently documented.

Ten negative physical-activity entries were treated as missing. Country was excluded from the analysis because 37.6% of entries were classified as “Other”.

These findings describe associations in the available data. They should not be interpreted as evidence that changing screen time, sleep or other habits would cause a change in mental health.

The takeaway

Look beyond the number of hours.

Students’ digital routines, daily habits and demographic contexts should be considered together. Better data documentation and further research are needed before making broader claims.
